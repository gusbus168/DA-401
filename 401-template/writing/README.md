How Solar Power Changed the Energy Business: Literature Review
Introduction
Solar power has moved from a marginal source of U.S. electricity to one of the fastest-growing segments of the energy sector, and the workforce tied to Solar has grown alongside it. Government reports capture these two sides through agencies such as the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), which documents how much electricity solar generates, while the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) document how many people work in the industry that produces it. What is missing from these sources is a direct comparison of two questions. This review looks at how the energy industry changed over the past decade as Solar has taken up a bigger share of the power grid and which parts of the industry have grown and shrunk? Second, has Solar's growth in generation kept up with its growth in jobs, or is one outpacing the other? This review summarizes the source data on Solar generation and solar employment and determines how the energy industry has changed as Solar has grown, and whether Solar's generation growth has matched its employment growth.
Makeup
Over the past decade, the structure of U.S. electricity generation has changed significantly. In 2015, EIA expected coal to supply about 36% of total U.S. generation and natural gas about 31%, with coal still the largest source that year (EIA, 2015a). By 2025, that flipped; coal's share of total utility-scale generation had dropped to roughly 17%, while natural gas's share had grown to about 40%, making gas the dominant fuel by a wide margin (EIA, 2026c). Nuclear has barely changed over the same period. Its share of total generation has stayed around 20% since 1990, mostly because the existing reactor fleet has seen few retirements or new builds (EIA, 2026c). Wind and Solar together went from a small share of the mix in 2015 to a record 17% of total U.S. generation in 2025 (EIA, 2026a). Utility-scale solar alone generated 296,000 gigawatt-hours in 2025 out of about 4.43 trillion kilowatt-hours of total utility-scale generation, or roughly 6.5% of the total (EIA, 2026a, 2026d), up from 1% ten years earlier (EIA, 2015b). So the decade's story is not only that Solar grew. Coal's share fell by half, natural gas expanded to cover most of that half, nuclear remained steady, and wind and solar went from a minor part of the grid to a major one.
Growth
Employment across the energy sector shifted along with the generation mix, but not always at the same rate. As early as 2014, BLS data showed the electric power generation sector losing jobs overall even as renewable generation jobs grew. The sector lost more than 5,800 net jobs between January 2011 and June 2014, mostly from coal plant closures, while non-hydro renewable jobs, led by Solar, grew by nearly 1,800 over the same period (EIA, 2014). Coal's job losses were the sharpest and most sustained of any part of the sector. U.S. coal mining employment fell from about 92,000 workers in 2011 to about 54,000 in 2018, a drop of roughly 42% in under a decade, and it has continued to shrink since (Ohio Manufacturers' Association, 2019). Natural gas employment grew over the same period, but not as much as its generation share would suggest, since combined-cycle gas plants need less work per unit of output than the coal plants they replaced. Solar and wind employment grew the fastest of any part of the sector, going from a small base in the early 2010s to about 370,600 solar workers and roughly 133,000 wind workers by 2024. That made Solar the largest subsector within Electric Power Generation (DOE, 2025). That growth broke in the most recent year of data available according to The 2026 USEER which found that Electric Power Generation employment overall fell about 2% (15,000 jobs) between 2024 and 2025, even as nuclear employment grew 4% (2,300 workers) and coal generation employment grew 5% (2,800 workers). Over that same year, solar employment fell about 3% (roughly 11,100 jobs) and wind fell about 2% (2,700 workers) (DOE, 2026).  The parts of the sector that lost jobs for most of the decade are coal, while nuclear gained workers in the most recent year and the part that grew fastest for most of the decade, Solar lost workers. This was the opposite of the decade-long pattern.
Over Time
Setting the decade-long picture aside, the same reversal shows up when comparing Solar's generation and employment numbers directly for the most recent year alone. EIA recorded 34% growth in utility-scale solar generation between 2024 and 2025, continuing a streak of annual increases that goes back to 2006 (EIA, 2026a). Over that same period, DOE's 2026 USEER found that solar employment fell by about 3% (DOE, 2026). Projections point the other way with BLS stating that employment in solar electric power generation will grow 180.2% between 2024 and 2034, the fastest projected rate of industries BLS tracks, and that employment of solar photovoltaic installers specifically will grow 37% over a comparable period (BLS, 2026a, 2026b). Neither the EIA generation trend or the USEER employment trend individually would predict what the other actually shows for the same year.  Generation climbing while the workforce producing it shrank. This doesn't prove a causality between the two though it could describe increasing labor productivity, workforce counts that miss contractors, a hiring pause, or something missing between building capacity and hiring for it. What it does show is that solar's generation growth and its employment growth are not moving together, which is the comparison the sources gathered do not make on their own.
Comparison

Comparing these trends draws on a few methods. The first is a descriptive trend tracking each energy source's percentage of total generation or total employment year over year. That's the method behind the decade-long shift described above. Time series method trend building on this by forecasting renewable energy variables from historical info and testing whether two series move together over time (Basmadjian et al., 2021). Other methods test the direction and movement of the relationship between renewable energy deployment and employment. Case studies outside the U.S. have applied that approach. Meta-analyses of this state that reported employment effects depend a lot on method. Studies that use input-output models, which account for indirect effects, report less optimistic job increases than simpler models (Stavropoulos & Burger, 2019). Input-output modeling through the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's Jobs and Economic Development Impact (JEDI) informs for turning a given level of solar investment or capacity into employment estimates (NREL, n.d.).  Lastly, survey-based census methods, like the one BW Research Partnership runs annually for USEER, and administrative data sources like QCEW, are still the primary way to count Solar workers.
Conclusion
Over the past decade, the U.S. energy industry changed with Solar's increased usage and incorporation into the U.S. grid. Coal's share of generation and its workforce both roughly halved. Natural gas absorbed most of coal's lost generation share, but its job growth lagged its output growth. Nuclear stayed essentially flat on both counts. Solar and wind employment grew faster than any other part of the sector for most of the decade, tracking their rising generation share. Within Solar specifically, generation growth has outpaced employment growth. EIA recorded 34% growth in utility-scale solar generation in 2025, while DOE's most recent employment data showed Solar employment falling about 3% over the same period. The two trends moved in the same direction for most of the decade but diverged in the most recent year, with output increasing as the workforce producing it shrank. So the answer to the second question is not that the two are moving together but that solar's growth in generation is currently outpacing its growth in jobs.
